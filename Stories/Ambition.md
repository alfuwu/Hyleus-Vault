Amine was a bright child. She had always been a bright child, learning how to speak at just one-and-a-half years old. She was currently learning how something called *multiplication* worked.

Math was strange. Amine—who oftentimes misspelled her name and referred to herself as "Amy"—had decided a while ago that she didn't quite like it.

It clicked with her, but it was *so* boring. She much rather enjoyed the occasional daydreams she had of whimsical places.

She most often dreamt of a place spewing black and white clouds into the blue sky. Metallic cylinders, hot air, and constant movement characterized those dreams. The people there spoke a language unlike any she had heard in person, one that her childish mind was slowly beginning to piece together from context clues.

She hadn't seen any of these "mah sheens," as the people from those dreams had called them, in any other place. It was part of the reason they enraptured her so—they tickled her curiosity for the unknown unlike the other, more boring daydreams did.

Other times, however, she dreamt of the Big Tree. Her immature mind couldn't even comprehend the Big Tree's massive scale; it was so gargantuan that the only word that came to her mind was "big". She liked those dreams a lot too. She got to see green people with leaves for hair and sharp teeth when she had those dreams, a sight she couldn't find anywhere else no matter how hard she looked around her village.

Really, the dreams were just better than the reality. Perhaps an older, more mature person would've found this to be depressing, but to Amy's—*Amine's*—childish innocence, she was simply happy to have them at all. She had asked other people about what kinds of dreams they had, and they all just stared at her in the same sort of confusion before slowly stating, as if she were somehow mentally deficient, that regular people "didn't have daydreams".

She would be loathe to not have the dreams. She didn't understand how the others lived without them!

She had even tried speaking some of the languages she'd heard from the dreams, with her limited understanding of them, but nobody could understand any of them.

It vexed Amy to no end. It was a puzzle with no solution; every possible answer she could conceive of was wrong.

They just didn't have the dreams.

She was... special. Being special made Amy happy. She liked being special. But sometimes it made her feel *other*. Left out. Excluded. That saddened her, and she had no idea how to reconcile the ostracization with the happiness that being unique brought.

She was just a child. An intelligent child, but a child nonetheless.
