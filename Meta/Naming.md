Simply coming up with random names for places might, on the surface, replicate Earth's seemingly random names for places, however Earth's cities, locations, countries, etc. are named as such due to three primary factors:
- **Place**
- **History**
- **People**

It is always one of these three factors that constitutes the original name for a place.

**Place**, as in some geographical feature (a lot of Earth names for geographical sights are redundant; one of the more prominent examples being the various rivers throughout Europe named the river "Avon", which literally means "river" in Welsh, so "river river"). This is the most common type of name, as it is used for less "significant" places.

**History**, as in some major historical event that occurred in the location or vicinity. This historical event doesn't necessarily need to even be true; many places are named after mythological feats or events that never actually occurred. An example of a historical place name is Seattle, which was named after the chieftain of the Duwamish tribe indigenous to the Seattle region in an act of respect.

However, the original name for some place is not necessarily the name it will have in the modern era. Names undergo change over time, the very same process that occurs with languages. The four major changes that can happen to a name are:
- **Evolution**
- **Simplification**
- **Conflation**
- **Elaboration**

Every name that is moderately old (>2 centuries) will most likely have undergone at least one of the above.

**Evolution** is the process in which a place may undergo from its base root into something distinctly unique. An example is the Castle of Gloucester, which has been changed to simply Gloucester over time.

**Simplification** is the act of removing modifiers from a place name, stripping the name down to the most important parts and leaving everything else behind. Additionally, it may also represent the removal or combination of syllables that are difficult to pronounce or easily skipped over. *Taupō-nui-a-Tia* is an example of this, being simply referred to as **Taupo** in modern times.

**Conflation** occurs when two words within the local language are similar and become *conflated* with one another. Their meanings can become bled together, creating a strange result, especially as the original word falls out of use. In Old English, *frith* means a woodland, and *firth* means a watery inlet. These two words are incredibly similar to one another, and were conflated together multiple times in historical maps.

**Elaboration** is when people add modifiers to a place name, not because the original name has lost its meaning, but to prevent it from being confused with a place that has a similar name. The most common example of this on Earth is the separation of several regions into "north" and "south", such as two of the States in America; North Dakota and South Dakota. A more niche application of elaboration is when it is applied without geographical descriptors, such as the City of London, located within London. People may also add geographical descriptors if the land itself has changed from what it used to be; some towns within Spain were named after flowers or trees that simply do not grow in those places anymore.

Finally, another factor to consider when naming something is who holds power in a given region. One of the more egregious examples of this is the Roman Empire during Caesar Julius's time; Caesar modified the entire calendar system to add two more months, one in his name (July) and another in Augustus's name (August).
Another example is Constantinople, now known as Istanbul. Constantinople was known as such for centuries, named after the Christian figure Constantine. However, when the Ottoman empire conquered Constantinople, the new population began to refer to the city as Istanbul rather than Constantinople, culminating in the city officially being renamed to Istanbul.

**Endonyms** and **exonyms** also have a role to play in the name a region is known by. An **endonym** is a name bestowed upon a place by the people living within it, and an **exonym** is a name given to a place by those who live outside of it. Most places are endonyms, however some, such as Scotland, which was a name coined by the Romans, are exonyms. Who holds power in a region also factors into which name sticks, as Scotland was likely referred to something more akin to Gaeland by the natives who lived there, but due to the Roman empire holding power over them as well as being the ones keeping historical records, Scotland was the name which stuck to the region. This means you shouldn't *just* consider what the region means to people living in it, you should also consider what it means to the people *not* living in it, and which of the two names will stick to the populace.

When designing a name for a region, town, city, or realm, all of the above factors should be considered.

Let's use these rules to explain the name of the Saviq'Qhast Ocean. The Saviq'Qhast Ocean was originally called the "Svalik-ju-Qhassut" by the indigenous population, meaning literally "water of the above", or, more contextually, "ocean of the divine". It was named as such in respect to their god, whom they believed resided beyond the edge of the Svalik-ju-Qhassut. However, this name eventually underwent simplification, transforming into the Svalik'Qhassut, and then further underwent changes when the Ckretion Empire conquered the various coastal peoples. To better fit the Ckretion lexicon, the name was changed to what it is now known as today, the Saviq'Qhast Ocean.
